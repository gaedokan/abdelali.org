When I interview a developer I focus on three important questions:

1. **Are they smart?**
2. **Can they get things done?**
3. **Can we (the team) work well with them?**

These questions are the core of my interview process. If a candidate doesn't meet all three, it can cause problems and frustration in the workplace.

## Are they smart?

When I ask "Are they smart?" I don't just mean intelligence or technical skills. In a developer interview, being smart means:

- **Problem-solving ability:** Can they solve problems creatively and logically? Do they break down challenges into smaller steps?
- **Adaptability:** Can they quickly learn new tools or concepts? Technology changes fast, so being able to adapt is very important.
- **Understanding of fundamentals:** Do they know the basic principles of programming well? It's not just about writing code but knowing why one approach is better than another.
- **Judgment:** Can they make good decisions when faced with different options? Being smart also means knowing when to keep things simple, or when to use proven methods instead of following the latest trend.

Being smart isn't just about knowing a lot. It's about how a person thinks, learns and applies their knowledge to real-world problems. But intelligence alone is not enough. A person might be smart, but if they can't get things done they won't contribute much to the team. They might give good advice, but that won't move the project forward.

## Can they get things done?

Getting things done is essential. Some people are smart but lack the focus or drive to finish tasks. On the other hand, someone who gets things done but isn't very smart might still succeed — but they often take a longer, more complicated path. This can slow down progress and cause frustration for everyone involved.

## Can we (the team) work well with them?

It's important that we can work well together. Even if someone is smart and productive, if we can't communicate and collaborate effectively it will be difficult to work together. A good, healthy working relationship is based on clear communication and respect.

## The problems with traditional interviews

Traditional developer interviews usually include:

- Reviewing a resume
- Asking tough questions
- Giving a coding problem

But I think this approach doesn't always work well:

- Resumes only tell you a little about a candidate's skills.
- Tough questions and coding problems often don't reflect real-world tasks, and even great programmers can struggle with them.

## My approach to interviews

To see if someone can really get things done, I generally focus on their past work. If a candidate has completed projects before, they can probably do it again. Today developers can easily get experience by contributing to open-source projects or building their own.

Here's how I do my interviews:

### 1. Request a code sample and a demo

Instead of just looking at resumes or asking difficult questions, I ask candidates to share a sample — or explain how it was built — and, if possible, a demo of something they've made. This shows me their actual work, not just how they perform in an artificial interview setting.

### 2. Evaluate the code

When I review the code, or the explanation of it, I look for clarity, conciseness, elegance and usability. I ask myself: *is this code something I would want in our product?* This helps me understand the candidate's skills and their approach to coding.

By focusing on these three questions and looking at real work instead of relying only on traditional interviews, I can better understand if a developer will fit well with the team. This approach leads to better results and a more enjoyable working environment.
